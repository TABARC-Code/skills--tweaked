# Precision Moves

A catalogue of move types for use after the web has been mapped. Each type names the thread it pulls, the typical effect, and the risk profile.

The point of a move catalogue is not to select from a menu. It is to recognise the shape of what you're doing before you do it — so you can be precise about it.

---

## Diagnostic Moves

**Purpose:** Create information before committing resources or relationships.

**The right question**
Ask the one question that, if answered honestly, would most change your understanding of the situation. Not the question you're supposed to ask — the one that would be uncomfortable to ask.
*Thread:* Hidden assumptions and unspoken knowledge.
*Risk:* The question may reveal something the asker preferred not to know.

**The minimum test**
Run a small, low-cost version of the proposed action and observe what happens. Does reality match the model?
*Thread:* Unknown unknowns.
*Risk:* Results may be treated as confirmation rather than information.

**The named assumption**
State an assumption explicitly and ask the person most likely to know if it's correct. Forces the assumption out of the background.
*Thread:* Framing capture and confident miscalibration.
*Risk:* Naming it may create commitment to it if the conversation goes poorly.

**The proxy signal**
Find something easily observable that would change if your hypothesis about the situation were correct. Watch it before acting.
*Thread:* Informational gaps.
*Risk:* Proxy may not be as correlated with the underlying condition as it appears.

## Structural Moves

**Purpose:** Change the shape of the system rather than managing its symptoms.

**The clarification**
Formally resolve an ambiguity — who has authority, what does "done" mean, who owns this. Often releases blockages nobody realised were authority questions.
*Thread:* Governance gaps and role conflict.
*Risk:* Clarity may force a conflict that ambiguity was papering over.

**The dependency removal**
Give something that depends on a fragile node an alternative path. Reduces systemic fragility without confronting the fragile node directly.
*Thread:* Overloaded nodes and sequence lock.
*Risk:* The alternative path may itself create a new dependency.

**The constraint challenge**
Identify a constraint being treated as fixed that is actually negotiable. Often requires only someone with authority to say so.
*Thread:* Premature closure and framing capture.
*Risk:* If the constraint turns out to be genuinely fixed, naming it as negotiable damages credibility.

**The role assignment**
Formally assign ownership of something currently owned by nobody or by too many people.
*Thread:* Governance gap and responsibility diffusion.
*Risk:* Assigning ownership can create accountability pressure where none existed, which some actors will resist.

## Relational Moves

**Purpose:** Change the dynamics between actors.

**The direct conversation**
Have the conversation everyone is avoiding. Most relational tension persists because nobody will name it. Name it carefully, specifically, and without accusation.
*Thread:* Trust deficit, historical injury, loyalty split.
*Risk:* Naming it removes the option of continued avoidance. Only use this if you're prepared for the outcome.

**The transparency move**
Share information that is currently asymmetric. Reduces suspicion; often releases defensive behaviour.
*Thread:* Communication asymmetry, trust deficit.
*Risk:* Information shared cannot be unshared. Consider who else will receive it by secondary transmission.

**The reframe**
Offer an interpretation of someone's behaviour that is more accurate — and often more charitable — than the current one circulating. If correct, it changes the response to that behaviour.
*Thread:* Status competition, historical injury.
*Risk:* If the reframe is wrong or is perceived as naive, it can damage the reframer's credibility.

**The third party**
Introduce a neutral party whose role is to hold the process, not the content. Useful when bilateral trust has degraded below the threshold needed for direct resolution.
*Thread:* Trust deficit, loyalty split.
*Risk:* Introduces a new actor with their own perspective and possible interests.

## Temporal Moves

**Purpose:** Change the timing dynamics of the situation.

**The urgency audit**
Ask whether the urgency is real, manufactured, or inherited. Trace it to its source. Often the original deadline has passed or changed without the downstream pressure dissipating.
*Thread:* Urgency vs. quality tension.
*Risk:* If the urgency is real, the audit costs time you don't have.

**The staged commitment**
Replace an all-or-nothing decision with a sequence of smaller decisions. Reduces pressure, creates information at each stage, preserves optionality.
*Thread:* Irreversibility pressure, short vs. long-term conflict.
*Risk:* Staging can be used to avoid commitment indefinitely; needs a clear endpoint.

**The pause**
Stop. Wait. Do nothing. Sometimes the system needs to stabilise before a move is productive. Acting into confusion rarely improves it.
*Thread:* Capacity overload, trust deficit in acute phase.
*Risk:* The pause may be read as weakness, indecision, or abandonment.

**The future anchor**
Commit to a decision at a specified future point rather than now. Reduces current pressure while preserving the option.
*Thread:* Urgency vs. quality, premature closure.
*Risk:* Can be used to defer indefinitely. Requires a genuine commitment to the anchor date.

## Move Selection Criteria

Before recommending a move, check:

**Reversibility** — Can you undo it if it's wrong? Strongly prefer reversible.

**Information value** — Does this move create useful information, or does it only change the situation? Moves that create information are generally lower risk.

**Grain alignment** — Does this work with the incentive structure or against it? Against the grain is harder, slower, and more likely to create resistance. It is sometimes necessary. Know which you're doing.

**Second-order effects** — What does this move enable or foreclose for later? The spider checks the web before moving across it.

**Minimum intervention** — Is there a smaller version of this move that achieves the same effect? If yes, start there.

**Who is doing the moving** — Some moves are only available to certain actors. The best move for the situation may not be available to the person asking. In that case, identify who could make the move and what it would take to get them there.
